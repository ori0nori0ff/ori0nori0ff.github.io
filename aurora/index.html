What is L1? The first Lagrange point: a spot 1.5 million km from Earth, straight towards the Sun, where the pull of the Sun and Earth balance so a spacecraft can stay put. NASA / NOAA probes there (DSCOVR, ACE, IMAP, SOHO) taste the solar wind 30–60 minutes before it reaches us — our early warning.

HOW TO READ IT ›

These are measurements from a spacecraft at L1, 1.5 million km towards the Sun — the solar wind it sees reaches Earth about 30–60 minutes later. Read it top to bottom:

Bz (red) — the most important line. When it goes south (below 0) and stays there, the solar wind's magnetic field connects with Earth's and pours energy in. Around −10 nT for a few hours → strong storm possible; −20 nT or lower → big storm. Short dips don't matter much; long, deep south Bz does.

Bt (white) — total strength of the field. Normal is 2–8 nT; 15–30+ nT usually means a CME has arrived. High Bt only becomes a storm when Bz turns south too.

Phi (purple) — which way the field points around the Sun. Jumps between ≈ 135° and ≈ 315° are the Earth crossing from one magnetic sector to another — often just before a fast stream. It doesn't drive the aurora by itself.

Density (orange) — particles per cm³; normal 2–10. A sudden jump in density, speed and Bt together is a CME shock arriving — storms can start within minutes to hours.

Speed (yellow) — normal 300–450 km/s. 500–700 = a fast stream from a coronal hole (minor storms, a few days). 700–1000+ = a strong CME.

Dashed marks — our guess from the shape of the lines: white = CME shock (speed, density and Bt jump in the same few minutes); green = coronal-hole stream (the density piles up first — orange mark — then speed and temperature climb slowly over a day). A hint for reading, not an official NOAA call.

Temperature (green) — rises together with speed in fast streams. Unusually cool wind at high speed is a sign of CME material.

For aurora from Romania you want all of it at once: speed 600+, Bt 20+, Bz −15 or lower for hours — then check the Kp and the sky.

How hard storms squeeze Earth's field

Loading…
Dst measures how much a storm weakens the magnetic field around the equator. Near 0 = calm; below −50 nT = a storm; below −100 = strong; below −250 = extreme (May 2024 reached −412).
Cosmic rays · neutron monitor · NMDB

Cosmic rays at the ground

Loading…
Particles from deep space hit the air and make neutrons that a monitor counts. When a CME passes Earth, its magnetic cloud sweeps some of them away: the count drops by a few percent within hours — a Forbush decrease, a real fingerprint of the Sun at the ground.

The horizontal pull of Earth's field at the nearest observatory with live data, against its 3-day middle. Calm days show only a slow daily wave of ~20–40 nT; a storm shakes it by 100 nT or more within hours. Surlari (Romania) reports a month late, so a neighbour is used. A rough guide.
