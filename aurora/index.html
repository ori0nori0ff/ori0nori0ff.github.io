HOW TO READ IT ›

These are measurements from a spacecraft at L1, 1.5 million km towards the Sun — the solar wind it sees reaches Earth about 30–60 minutes later. Read it top to bottom:

Bz (red) — the most important line. When it goes south (below 0) and stays there, the solar wind's magnetic field connects with Earth's and pours energy in. Around −10 nT for a few hours → strong storm possible; −20 nT or lower → big storm. Short dips don't matter much; long, deep south Bz does.

Bt (white) — total strength of the field. Normal is 2–8 nT; 15–30+ nT usually means a CME has arrived. High Bt only becomes a storm when Bz turns south too.

Phi (purple) — which way the field points around the Sun. Jumps between ≈ 135° and ≈ 315° are the Earth crossing from one magnetic sector to another — often just before a fast stream. It doesn't drive the aurora by itself.

Density (orange) — particles per cm³; normal 2–10. A sudden jump in density, speed and Bt together is a CME shock arriving — storms can start within minutes to hours.

Speed (yellow) — normal 300–450 km/s. 500–700 = a fast stream from a coronal hole (minor storms, a few days). 700–1000+ = a strong CME.

Temperature (green) — rises together with speed in fast streams. Unusually cool wind at high speed is a sign of CME material.

For aurora from Romania you want all of it at once: speed 600+, Bt 20+, Bz −15 or lower for hours — then check the Kp and the sky.

Your sky

The last step is local: dark, clear, the Moon out of the way and a view to the north. The answer card at the top puts it all together for tonight.

How to read all this

A good aurora night needs

  1. Kp 7+ forecast for tonight where you are (the answer card says it for you).
  2. Bz negative (south) for an hour or more — that's the "on" switch.
  3. Fast wind, over 500 km/s, or a CME arriving (red band).
  4. Clear sky and real dark: away from city lights, facing north, low horizon.
  5. Use your phone's night mode (3–10 s exposure). From Romania the aurora often looks like a faint grey-pink glow to the eye but shows red / green on camera.
WHY IS IT SO RARE IN THE SOUTH?

The aurora lives in a ring around the North Pole (the oval above). Romania and southern Europe are far south of it. Only big solar storms push the ring far enough south — a few nights per year, more around solar maximum (now, 2024–2027).
